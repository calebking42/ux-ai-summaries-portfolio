UX Pilot Study

AI Summaries in Google Search:
Does Visibility Lead to Verification?

Google now answers many searches before showing any links. Do people still check where the answers come from?

By Caleb King · GRC-4290 User Experience Research Methods · Instructor: Hocheol Yang · October 13, 2026

The Setup

An observation session plus a short interview with one participant.

Method

Observation plus a short follow-up interview.

Who

"Participant A" — a frequent AI user, on an Apple device, using Google search.

How

Six tasks read aloud, one at a time. Each task was timed, and after each one he rated his confidence from 1–5.

Controls

I confirmed beforehand that each query produced an accurate AI summary. He didn't know the topic of the study until afterward.

Framework: Nielsen's visibility of system status.

Key Findings

Click a card to expand it.

5/5 Maximum confidence, every time

Confidence was 5 out of 5 on every task, including the health and money questions.

0 Zero sources opened

He never scrolled below the AI summary and opened zero sources across all six tasks.

28s The parking-pass stumble

The parking-pass task took longest (28 seconds) because the summary gave two answers. He reported both instead of checking which one was correct.

✓ He likes it this way

He said he preferred having the summary, and that if an answer were wrong he'd open the first source or two.

Why It Matters

  • Nielsen (1994), visibility of system status: interfaces should keep users informed, and clear communication builds trust. Source links were visible, but he never used them.
  • Pew Research (Chapekis & Lieb, 2025): people clicked a regular result link in 8% of visits with an AI summary vs. 15% without, and clicked a link inside the summary in only 1%.
  • Knor et al. (2026): verification prompts and cited sources did not make people more likely to verify; people mainly checked when they already doubted the answer.
  • Li & Aral (2025, preprint): citations raised trust in AI answers even when incorrect.

Limitations

  • One participant — and a heavy AI user at that.
  • Hypothetical tasks with no real stakes.
  • All summaries were accurate, so I couldn't observe a response to an error.

What I'd Test Next

  • A skeptical or low-AI user.
  • A task with real stakes.
  • A summary that is wrong.

My Take

AI summaries make search faster and he valued that — but visible links alone didn't lead him to check anything. Google should do more to prompt verification on health and money topics, where a wrong answer costs the most.
